# Cover Cropping

## Purpose

A cover crop is grown to protect and improve the soil rather than to be
harvested. Between two cash crops it occupies the window when the surface would
otherwise be bare, which is exactly when erosion, evaporation and weed
establishment do the most damage.

The benefits accumulate slowly. A single cover crop season produces a modest
change in organic matter; a decade of consistent cover cropping changes the
infiltration behaviour of the soil.

## What it does

Living roots hold aggregates together and keep the surface rough enough to slow
runoff. On light sandy soils this is the difference between a topsoil that stays
put over winter and one that moves with the first heavy rain.

Canopy cover suppresses weeds by competing for light and nitrogen during the
fallow. A dense winter cover is one of the few non-chemical measures that
reliably suppresses wild oat establishment in a cereal rotation.

Root channels and added residue raise organic matter and feed soil biology, which
over time improves aggregate stability and water-holding capacity.

## Biofumigation

Brassica cover crops such as mustard and radish accumulate glucosinolates. When
the tissue is macerated and incorporated into moist soil, these break down into
isothiocyanates, compounds that suppress root-knot nematode juveniles and some
soilborne fungi. The effect depends on fine chopping and immediate incorporation;
a brassica cover left to dry on the surface provides little of it.

## The water cost

In semi-arid regions a cover crop uses stored soil water that the following cash
crop would otherwise have. Where the annual rainfall is near 300 mm, as in the
Konya basin, this cost can outweigh the soil benefit and the cover must be
terminated early to limit transpiration. Cover cropping is a straightforward
recommendation on the wetter Aegean and Black Sea soils and a conditional one on
the Anatolian plateau.
