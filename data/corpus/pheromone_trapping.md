# Pheromone Trapping

## Two distinct uses

Monitoring traps are deployed at low density, typically one or two per hectare, to
detect when a flight begins and how large it is. They do not reduce the
population; they time the decision. Catch data converts an unknown into a spray
window, and in many systems the first sustained catch starts a degree-day count
toward the treatment date.

Mass trapping uses a much higher density, often 20 to 100 traps per hectare, with
the aim of removing enough adults to suppress the next generation. Here the traps
are the control measure.

## Where mass trapping works

It works best against a pest with a single dominant host, limited immigration from
outside the block, and a female that mates once. The olive fruit fly meets all
three conditions, and mass trapping in olive groves can hold infestation below the
treatment threshold across a season without insecticide, provided the whole block
is covered and traps are serviced on schedule.

It works poorly where adults immigrate continuously from surrounding untreated
areas, because the trapped population is replaced as fast as it is removed.

## Mating disruption

A related approach saturates the block with synthetic pheromone so that males
cannot locate females against the background. This suppresses successful
oviposition rather than removing adults, and is used against corn borer in maize.
It shares the same weakness: it requires area-wide application, since a small
treated block surrounded by untreated fields simply receives already-mated
females.

## Practical requirements

Lures degrade and must be replaced on the manufacturer's schedule; an expired lure
produces falling catches that look like a declining population and invite exactly
the wrong conclusion. Traps need servicing, and catch records are only useful if
they are kept consistently enough to compare across weeks.
