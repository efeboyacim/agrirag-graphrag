# Soil Solarization

## Method

Soil solarization covers moist, cultivated soil with transparent polyethylene
film during the hottest part of summer. Solar radiation passes through the film
and the trapped heat raises soil temperature well above what bare soil reaches,
typically to 45-50 C in the upper 10 centimetres and 38-42 C deeper in the
profile.

Three conditions are non-negotiable: the film must be transparent rather than
black, because black film absorbs heat at the surface instead of transmitting it;
the soil must be wet, because moist soil conducts heat downward and wet
pathogen structures are far more heat-sensitive than dry ones; and the treatment
must run for four to six weeks during peak summer.

## What it controls

Root-knot nematode juveniles in the upper profile are killed outright.
Verticillium microsclerotia are substantially inactivated in the tilled layer,
which is one of the few practical interventions against a pathogen that rotation
suppresses only over many years. Many soilborne fungi and a large fraction of the
weed seed bank in the heated layer are also controlled.

## Limits

The effect is depth-limited. Pathogen structures below roughly 30 centimetres
survive and will recolonise the treated layer over time, so solarization is a
suppression tool with a finite duration rather than an eradication.

It also requires taking the field out of production during the summer, which means
it fits a fallow period in a rotation rather than an intensive continuous
cropping system. Film cost and disposal are real, and the technique only works in
regions with reliably hot, cloudless summers - the Mediterranean and southeastern
regions qualify, the Black Sea coast does not.

## Combination effects

Solarization pairs well with biological amendments. Introducing Trichoderma after
solarization colonises a partially sterilised soil with little competition, which
establishes the antagonist far more effectively than application into an intact
soil biology.
