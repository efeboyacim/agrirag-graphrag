# No-Till Direct Seeding

## The practice

No-till seeds directly into undisturbed soil through the previous crop's residue.
A specialised drill cuts a narrow slot, places seed and fertiliser, and closes it,
leaving the rest of the surface untouched. The residue stays on top as a mulch.

## Why it conserves water

An undisturbed, residue-covered surface loses far less water to evaporation than
a freshly ploughed one. Tillage exposes moist soil to air and breaks the
continuity of pores; both accelerate drying. In rainfed cereal systems where the
crop lives on moisture stored over winter, this retained water translates
directly into yield stability in a dry spring.

The residue cover also protects against wind erosion, which is the dominant
degradation process on the exposed calcareous plains of Central Anatolia.

## Structure and biology

Leaving the profile undisturbed allows earthworm channels and old root pores to
persist from season to season. These continuous vertical pores conduct water
downward far more effectively than the disconnected porosity created by tillage,
so infiltration usually improves after the first few years.

## The transition problem

No-till is not free. Weed control shifts from mechanical to chemical in the early
years, because tillage was previously doing part of that work. Residue can
harbour pathogens that ploughing would have buried, which raises the importance
of rotation and resistant cultivars. Compaction that already exists will not
disappear on its own and may need a single remedial subsoiling before the system
is established.

Yields commonly dip for two or three seasons before matching and then exceeding
the tilled baseline. Farmers who abandon no-till usually do so during that dip.
