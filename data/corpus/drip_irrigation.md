# Drip Irrigation

## How it differs

Drip irrigation delivers water through emitters directly onto or into the soil at
the plant row, wetting only part of the surface. Surface and sprinkler systems
wet everything. That difference drives most of the agronomic consequences.

## Efficiency

Because the wetted area is small and the canopy stays dry, evaporative losses are
much lower than under sprinklers, and there is no runoff or deep percolation from
over-application at the field edges. Application efficiency of 85 to 95 percent is
routine, against 60 to 75 percent for well-managed furrow irrigation.

Frequent small applications also keep soil water in a narrow band near field
capacity, avoiding the wet-dry cycling that stresses the crop between furrow
irrigations.

## Disease and weed side effects

A dry canopy is a hostile environment for foliar fungal pathogens that need leaf
wetness to germinate. Switching from sprinkler to drip reduces infection periods
for several leaf diseases without any fungicide.

Leaving the inter-row dry also suppresses weed germination there, which cuts
herbicide requirements in widely spaced row crops such as cotton.

## Fertigation

Drip lines can carry soluble fertiliser, allowing nutrients to be split across
many small applications timed to uptake demand. This is the most practical way to
implement split nitrogen in a row crop, and it reduces leaching losses compared
with one or two large broadcast applications.

## Costs and failure modes

Capital cost per hectare is high and emitters clog. Water with high bicarbonate
or iron content precipitates inside the emitter, and organic matter or algae
blocks it biologically. Filtration and periodic acid or chlorine flushing are not
optional extras; a neglected system loses uniformity within a season and the
resulting patchy field is worse than a uniform furrow-irrigated one.
