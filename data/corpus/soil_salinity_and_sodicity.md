# Salinity and Sodicity

## Two different problems

Salinity and sodicity are routinely confused. Salinity is a matter of total
dissolved salts: the soil solution becomes concentrated enough that the plant
must expend energy to extract water, producing symptoms that look like drought in
a wet soil.

Sodicity is about which cation dominates the exchange sites. When sodium occupies
a high proportion of them, clay particles repel each other and aggregates
disperse. The soil loses structure, seals at the surface, and stops conducting
water.

The distinction matters because the remedies are different and applying the wrong
one makes things worse.

## Fixing salinity

Salinity is removed by leaching. Apply water in excess of crop demand so the
wetting front carries salts below the root zone, and ensure the drainage exists to
take that water away. Leaching into a field with a high water table simply
recycles the salt back up by capillary rise in the dry season.

## Fixing sodicity

Leaching alone makes a sodic soil worse, because removing salts while sodium still
dominates the exchange complex accelerates dispersion. Sodium must first be
displaced by calcium. Agricultural gypsum supplies soluble calcium that exchanges
for sodium on the clay surface; the displaced sodium is then leached away.

Gypsum is therefore the standard amendment for the heavy sodic clays of irrigated
plains, and the sequence matters: amend first, leach second.

## Prevention

Irrigation water quality determines the long-run trajectory. Water with a high
sodium adsorption ratio pushes a soil toward sodicity over years regardless of
management. Combining a deficit irrigation schedule with saline water is
particularly risky, because the reduced application also reduces the leaching that
would otherwise hold salts in check.
