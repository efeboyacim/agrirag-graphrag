# Soil pH and Nutrient Availability

## Why pH dominates nutrition

Soil pH does not feed a plant, but it governs the chemical form of almost every
nutrient and therefore whether the plant can take it up. A soil can be rich in
total phosphorus or iron and still starve the crop of both if the pH is wrong.

Most nutrients are collectively most available between pH 6.0 and 7.0, which is
why that band is the target for the majority of field crops.

## The alkaline problem

Above about pH 7.5, phosphorus precipitates as calcium phosphates and becomes
progressively unavailable. Micronutrient cations - iron, zinc, manganese and
copper - form insoluble hydroxides and carbonates.

Iron deficiency chlorosis on calcareous soils is the classic symptom: interveinal
yellowing of young leaves in a soil that contains abundant total iron. Adding more
iron to the soil rarely fixes it, because the added iron precipitates as fast as
the native iron. Chelated foliar applications bypass the soil chemistry entirely
and are the practical remedy.

The calcareous soils of Central Anatolia and parts of the Aegean sit in this
range, with pH commonly between 7.8 and 8.6, and zinc deficiency in cereals is
widespread there.

## The acid problem

Below about pH 5.5, aluminium becomes soluble and is directly toxic to root tips,
stunting the root system and limiting water and nutrient capture. Calcium and
magnesium are depleted and molybdenum becomes unavailable.

The acidic forest soils of the Eastern Black Sea, with pH between 4.5 and 6.0,
sit here. Hazelnut tolerates this range well, which is one reason the crop and the
region fit each other so precisely.

## Changing pH

Raising pH means liming, and the requirement depends on buffering capacity rather
than the current pH alone; a clay soil needs far more lime than a sand to move the
same distance. Lowering pH on a calcareous soil is usually impractical because the
free carbonate buffers any acidifying input. Acidifying fertilisers such as
ammonium sulfate acidify the immediate fertiliser band, which helps locally, but
they do not shift whole-field pH against a carbonate buffer.
