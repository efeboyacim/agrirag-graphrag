# Southeastern Anatolia and the GAP Region

## The setting

The Southeastern Anatolia region around Sanliurfa and the Harran plain has a hot
semi-arid climate: summer temperatures routinely exceed 40 C, winters are mild,
and annual rainfall of about 450 mm falls almost entirely in winter. The mean
annual temperature is near 18.5 C.

Soils are predominantly clay loams with calcareous areas, deep and inherently
fertile.

## Transformation by irrigation

The Southeastern Anatolia Project brought large-scale surface irrigation to a
plain that had previously supported only winter cereals and pulses. Cotton and
maize followed immediately, and yields are high - seed cotton around 4.5 t/ha,
maize near 8.8 t/ha.

That transformation also created the region's characteristic soil problem. Surface
irrigation applied to flat land with limited natural drainage raised water tables
and brought salts toward the root zone. Salinity and, in places, sodicity have
developed on land that was not previously affected.

## Managing the salt

The remedy separates into two distinct interventions that are frequently confused.
Where the problem is accumulated soluble salts, the answer is a leaching fraction
plus functioning subsurface drainage; leaching into a field with a high water
table simply recycles salt upward in the dry season.

Where sodium dominates the exchange complex, gypsum must displace it before
leaching will help, and leaching first makes the structural problem worse.

Conversion from surface to drip irrigation reduces both the applied volume and the
water table pressure, though it requires that a leaching fraction still be
budgeted.

## Pest pressure

The hot summers favour cotton bollworm and fall armyworm, both of which are
established in the region's summer crops. Sunn pest affects wheat in spring. The
combination of high temperatures and irrigation produces multiple pest generations
per season, which raises both the number of interventions and the resistance
selection pressure.
