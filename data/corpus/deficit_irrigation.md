# Deficit Irrigation

## The idea

Deficit irrigation deliberately supplies less water than the crop would use under
full evapotranspiration demand. The goal is not maximum yield per hectare but
maximum yield per unit of water, which is the relevant objective wherever water
rather than land is the limiting resource.

## Why it works

Crop yield does not respond linearly to water. Early and late in the season the
response is shallow: a mild shortfall costs little. Around flowering and early
grain or boll filling the response is steep, and a shortfall there is expensive.

Deficit irrigation exploits that asymmetry. Water is withheld during the tolerant
stages and concentrated on the sensitive ones. A well-designed schedule can cut
applied water by a quarter for a yield loss in the single-digit percentages.

## Where it fits

In Central Anatolian wheat, the sensitive window runs from booting through early
grain filling. Irrigation applied there protects grain number and weight; water
applied during late vegetative growth mostly grows straw.

In cotton, the flowering and boll-filling period is the critical window.
Restricting water during early vegetative growth also has a useful side effect:
it limits excessive vegetative growth that would otherwise shade the lower canopy
and complicate harvest.

## The prerequisite

Deficit irrigation requires knowing the soil water status, not guessing it.
Without soil moisture monitoring or a reliable evapotranspiration estimate, a
deliberate deficit is indistinguishable from accidental drought stress, and the
technique quietly becomes simple under-irrigation.

## Interaction with salinity

Applying less water means applying less leaching. In regions where irrigation
water carries a significant salt load, a deficit schedule must still budget a
periodic leaching fraction, or salts accumulate in the root zone over successive
seasons.
